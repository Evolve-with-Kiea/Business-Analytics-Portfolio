# 1. Purpose

The Excel Financial Analysis folder is designed to explore how financial information can be organized, calculated, compared, and interpreted to understand business performance.

Financial statements and accounting records contain valuable information about revenue, expenses, profitability, liquidity, and financial efficiency.

However, financial data becomes more useful when it is transformed into meaningful comparisons, ratios, trends, and management reports.

For example, an income statement may show that revenue increased during a reporting period.

Financial analysis can help investigate whether:

- Gross profit increased proportionately.
- Operating expenses grew faster than revenue.
- Profit margins improved or declined.
- Results differed from budget expectations.
- Financial performance changed across periods.

The goal is to develop the ability to move beyond recording financial transactions and toward interpreting what financial information reveals about business performance.

### Central Business Question

**How can Microsoft Excel be used to analyze financial statements, evaluate profitability, compare budgets with actual results, calculate financial ratios, and communicate meaningful financial performance insights?**

# 4. Understanding Financial Statements

Financial statements provide structured information about a business's financial position and performance.

### Income Statement

Reports revenue, expenses, and profit or loss over a defined period.

### Balance Sheet

Reports assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time.

### Statement of Cash Flows

Reports cash inflows and outflows from operating, investing, and financing activities.

### Important Principle

**The income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement answer different financial questions.**

For example, a profitable business may still experience cash flow difficulties.

Financial analysis should distinguish profitability, liquidity, and cash movement.

# 6. Revenue Analysis

## Purpose

Evaluate revenue by period, product, segment, or other relevant classification.

### Potential Metrics

- Total revenue
- Revenue by category
- Revenue by month
- Revenue growth rate
- Revenue share by segment

### Revenue Growth Formula

**Revenue Growth % = (Current Period Revenue − Previous Period Revenue) ÷ Previous Period Revenue × 100**

### Illustrative Example

A fictional business reports:

- Previous period revenue: $80,000
- Current period revenue: $100,000

**Revenue Growth = ($100,000 − $80,000) ÷ $80,000 × 100 = 25%**

### Interpretation

Revenue increased by 25% between the two periods.

This does not establish whether profitability or cash flow improved.

### Important Consideration

Revenue definitions should be consistent across reporting periods.

# 8. Gross Profit Analysis

## Purpose

Evaluate profitability after accounting for cost of goods sold.

### Formula

**Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold**

### Illustrative Example

A fictional business reports:

- Revenue: $100,000
- Cost of goods sold: $40,000

**Gross Profit = $60,000**

### Business Questions

- Is gross profit increasing?
- How do direct costs affect financial performance?
- Are revenue gains translating into stronger gross profit?
- Are product costs changing over time?

### Important Consideration

Gross profit does not account for all operating and financing expenses.

---

# 9. Gross Profit Margin

## Purpose

Measure gross profit as a percentage of revenue.

### Formula

**Gross Margin % = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

### Illustrative Example

- Gross profit: $60,000
- Revenue: $100,000

**Gross Margin = 60%**

### Interpretation

Under this simplified example, 60 cents of gross profit remain for each dollar of revenue before operating and other expenses.

### Potential Business Questions

- Is gross margin improving?
- Are production costs increasing?
- How does gross margin compare across periods?
- Are margins affected by changes in sales mix?

# 10. Operating Profit Analysis

## Purpose

Evaluate profitability from operating activities.

### Simplified Formula

**Operating Profit = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses**

### Illustrative Example

- Gross profit: $60,000
- Operating expenses: $35,000

**Operating Profit = $25,000**

### Important Consideration

Actual financial statements may include additional operating income, expenses, or classifications.

The simplified formula should be adapted to the source statement.

# 12. Net Profit Analysis

## Purpose

Evaluate profit after applicable expenses and other items.

### Simplified Formula

**Net Profit = Total Revenue and Gains − Total Expenses and Losses**

### Illustrative Example

**Net Profit = $18,000**

### Important Consideration

Actual net income calculations depend on the complete financial statement and applicable accounting treatment.

# 14. Budget Versus Actual Analysis

## Purpose

Compare planned financial performance with recorded results.

### Potential Measures

- Budgeted revenue
- Actual revenue
- Budgeted expenses
- Actual expenses
- Dollar variance
- Percentage variance

### Dollar Variance

**Variance = Actual − Budget**

### Percentage Variance

**Variance % = (Actual − Budget) ÷ Budget × 100**

### Illustrative Example

| Financial Item | Budget | Actual | Variance |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | $50,000 | $55,000 | +$5,000 |
| Marketing Expense | $5,000 | $6,000 | +$1,000 |
| Administrative Expense | $8,000 | $7,500 | −$500 |

### Interpretation

- Revenue exceeded budget by $5,000.
- Marketing expense exceeded budget by $1,000.
- Administrative expense was $500 below budget.

### Important Consideration

A positive variance is not always favorable.

Revenue and expense variances require different interpretations.

# 16. Financial Ratio Analysis

Financial ratios provide standardized measures for evaluating financial condition and performance.

### Main Ratio Categories

| Category | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Liquidity | Ability to meet short-term obligations |
| Profitability | Ability to generate earnings |
| Efficiency | Effectiveness of asset and resource use |
| Leverage | Extent of financing through debt |
| Coverage | Ability to meet selected financial obligations |

Ratios should be interpreted alongside financial statement context, historical performance, and appropriate benchmarks.

---

# 17. Liquidity Ratios

## Current Ratio

**Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities**

### Illustrative Example

- Current assets: $120,000
- Current liabilities: $60,000

**Current Ratio = 2.0**

### Interpretation

The fictional business has $2 of current assets for each $1 of current liabilities.

A higher current ratio does not automatically indicate stronger overall financial health.

### Quick Ratio

**Quick Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents + Short-Term Marketable Securities + Net Receivables) ÷ Current Liabilities**

The exact definition should be documented.

---

# 18. Profitability Ratios

### Gross Margin

Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100

### Operating Margin

Operating Profit ÷ Revenue × 100

### Net Profit Margin

Net Profit ÷ Revenue × 100

### Return on Assets

**ROA = Net Income ÷ Average Total Assets × 100**

### Return on Equity

**ROE = Net Income ÷ Average Shareholders' Equity × 100**

### Important Consideration

Financial ratios may use average balance sheet values when comparing period income with assets or equity.

---

# 19. Efficiency Ratios

## Asset Turnover

**Asset Turnover = Net Revenue ÷ Average Total Assets**

## Receivables Turnover

**Receivables Turnover = Net Credit Sales ÷ Average Net Accounts Receivable**

## Inventory Turnover

**Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory**

### Potential Business Questions

- How efficiently are assets used?
- How quickly are receivables collected?
- How frequently is inventory sold?
- Are efficiency measures changing over time?

### Important Consideration

Ratios should use compatible reporting periods and accounting definitions.

---

# 20. Leverage Ratios

## Debt-to-Equity Ratio

**Debt-to-Equity = Defined Total Debt ÷ Total Equity**

### Potential Business Questions

- How does the business finance its operations?
- Is financial leverage changing?
- How do debt levels compare with equity?

### Important Consideration

Debt definitions vary.

The selected calculation should specify whether it includes interest-bearing debt only or broader liabilities.

# 22. Horizontal Analysis

Horizontal analysis compares financial statement amounts across periods.

### Formula

**Percentage Change = (Current Period − Base Period) ÷ Base Period × 100**

### Potential Applications

- Revenue changes
- Expense changes
- Asset changes
- Liability changes
- Profit changes

### Important Consideration

The selected base period must be identified.

Percentage changes may be undefined or misleading when the base amount is zero or changes sign.

---

# 23. Vertical Analysis

Vertical analysis expresses financial statement items as percentages of a common base.

### Income Statement Example

**Expense Percentage = Expense Amount ÷ Revenue × 100**

### Balance Sheet Example

**Asset Category Percentage = Asset Category Amount ÷ Total Assets × 100**

### Business Questions

- What proportion of revenue is consumed by operating expenses?
- How has the expense structure changed?
- What proportion of assets is held in cash or receivables?

# 30. Interpretation Expectations

Every completed analysis should explain:

- What the metric measures.
- How the metric was calculated.
- Whether the result increased or decreased.
- Why the change may matter.
- What the available data supports.
- Which assumptions influence interpretation.
- What limitations remain.

### Example Interpretation

A fictional company's gross margin decreases from 45% to 40%.

A responsible interpretation would identify the five-percentage-point decrease and investigate potential causes such as product costs, pricing, or changes in sales mix.

The decrease alone does not prove which factor caused the change.
