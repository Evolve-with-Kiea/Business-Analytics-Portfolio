# Business Problems

## Business Problem Identification, Analytical Scoping & Case Study Development

### Purpose

This folder represents the first stage of my business analytics case study development process: identifying, defining, and documenting a meaningful business problem before beginning data analysis.

The objective is to develop a structured approach to understanding business challenges, translating broad concerns into measurable questions, identifying decision-making requirements, and establishing clear expectations for analytical work.

My professional background includes more than 20 years of experience in accounting and financial operations, including accounts receivable, accounts payable, bookkeeping, reconciliations, cash applications, and enterprise business systems.

As I pursue an Associate of Applied Science in Business Analytics at Wake Technical Community College, I am expanding that experience through business problem-solving, analytical reasoning, data interpretation, and technical skill development.

This folder provides a consistent framework for approaching potential business challenges in financial management, accounting operations, sales, marketing, e-commerce, organizational performance, and business process improvement.

**Core principle: Effective business analysis begins with understanding the problem, not choosing the software.**

---

## 1. Why Business Problem Definition Matters

A business may have access to large amounts of information without knowing which questions to ask or which decisions the information should support.

For example, a company may know that sales are declining but may not understand whether the issue relates to:

- Changes in customer purchasing behavior
- Product pricing
- Inventory availability
- Marketing effectiveness
- Customer retention
- Seasonal demand
- Increased competition
- Operational challenges

Creating a dashboard without first defining the business problem may produce attractive visualizations that do not address the underlying concern.

A well-defined business problem establishes:

1. What is happening.
2. Why the issue matters.
3. Who is affected.
4. What decision needs to be made.
5. Which questions require investigation.
6. What information may be needed.
7. How analytical success will be evaluated.

This approach supports focused, relevant, and decision-oriented analysis.

## 2. Business Problem Development Framework

Each case study should progress through the following stages.

| Stage | Primary Question | Expected Output |
|---|---|---|
| Problem Identification | What business issue needs attention? | Problem statement |
| Business Context | What is happening within the organization? | Background and scenario |
| Stakeholder Identification | Who needs the analysis? | Stakeholder description |
| Business Impact | Why does the issue matter? | Impact assessment |
| Objective Definition | What should the analysis accomplish? | Business objectives |
| Research Questions | What specifically needs to be answered? | Analytical questions |
| Data Requirements | What information is needed? | Preliminary data requirements |
| Scope Definition | What is included and excluded? | Scope statement |
| Success Criteria | What would make the analysis useful? | Evaluation criteria |
| Decision Alignment | How will findings support action? | Intended decision |

This framework establishes the foundation for subsequent data collection, preparation, analysis, visualization, and recommendation development.

## 3. Identifying a Business Problem

A useful business problem should be specific enough to investigate and meaningful enough to support a decision.

### Broad Concern vs. Defined Business Problem

| Broad Concern | More Specific Business Problem |
|---|---|
| Sales are declining. | Monthly revenue has declined across selected product categories, and management needs to identify the primary contributing factors. |
| Customers are not paying. | Overdue accounts receivable balances are increasing, and the finance team needs to identify aging patterns and customer concentration. |
| Marketing is expensive. | Marketing expenses are increasing, but management cannot determine which channels generate sufficient returns. |
| The business is not profitable. | Revenue is growing while operating margins are declining, requiring an investigation of costs and profitability. |
| Operations are inefficient. | Invoice processing delays are affecting payment cycles, and management needs to identify workflow bottlenecks. |
| Customers are leaving. | Repeat purchasing activity appears to be declining, and the business needs to evaluate customer retention patterns. |

The more specific version establishes an analytical direction without assuming the cause.

### Problem Identification Questions

Before beginning a case study, consider:

- What issue has been observed?
- What evidence suggests the issue exists?
- Is the problem ongoing or temporary?
- Which business function is affected?
- What financial or operational consequences may result?
- Who is responsible for the decision?
- What is currently unknown?
- What would management do differently if the analysis answered the question?

## 4. Business Context & Scenario Classification

Every case study must identify the nature of the business scenario.

### Scenario Types

| Classification | Description |
|---|---|
| Hypothetical Business | A fictional business created for analytical learning |
| Synthetic Data Scenario | A business scenario supported by artificially generated records |
| Public Data Case Study | An analysis based on publicly available information |
| Personal Business Scenario | An analysis involving information I am authorized to use |
| Anonymized Scenario | A case study using appropriately de-identified information with necessary authorization |

### Required Scenario Information

Each case study should explain:

- Type of organization
- Industry or business model
- Products or services
- Relevant business process
- Operating environment
- Business challenge
- Stakeholders
- Available information
- Scenario classification
- Assumptions

**Integrity Standard:** Hypothetical scenarios must not be presented as actual client engagements, employer projects, or verified business results.

## 5. Defining Business Objectives

Business objectives explain what the analysis is intended to accomplish.

Strong objectives should be:

- Specific
- Relevant
- Measurable where possible
- Realistic given available information
- Connected to a business decision

### Example: Accounts Receivable

**Business Problem:** A hypothetical company has experienced an increase in overdue customer balances.

**Potential Objectives:**

1. Determine the proportion of outstanding receivables in each aging category.
2. Identify customers or categories contributing most to overdue balances.
3. Compare aging patterns across selected reporting periods.
4. Develop recommendations for further collection review.

### Example: E-commerce Profitability

**Business Problem:** A hypothetical online retailer does not know which product categories generate the strongest margins.

**Potential Objectives:**

1. Calculate revenue, costs, and gross profit by product category.
2. Compare gross profit margins across categories.
3. Identify products with high sales but relatively low margins.
4. Provide information to support product and pricing decisions.

Objectives should not promise outcomes that the available data cannot support.

## 6. Developing Research Questions

Research questions translate the business problem into specific analytical tasks.

### Effective Research Questions

Research questions should:

- Address the stated problem.
- Be answerable using available or reasonably obtainable data.
- Avoid assuming an explanation before analysis.
- Identify relevant metrics or comparisons.
- Support a potential business decision.

### Examples

**Financial Analysis**

- Which expense categories account for the largest share of operating costs?
- How have profit margins changed across reporting periods?
- Which customers contribute the largest overdue receivable balances?

**Sales Analysis**

- Which products generate the highest revenue?
- How do sales patterns vary by month?
- Which categories have the strongest gross profit margins?

**Marketing Analysis**

- Which marketing channels generate the most attributable conversions?
- How does customer acquisition cost vary by channel?
- Which campaigns demonstrate stronger returns based on available data?

**Operations Analysis**

- Which workflow stages experience the longest delays?
- How does processing time vary by transaction type?
- Where do errors or rework occur most frequently?

**Customer Analysis**

- What proportion of customers make repeat purchases?
- How does purchase frequency vary by customer segment?
- Which customer groups contribute the greatest revenue?

## 7. Identifying Stakeholders & Decision-Makers

Business analysis should consider who needs the information and how it will be used.

| Stakeholder | Potential Analytical Need |
|---|---|
| Business Owner | Overall performance and profitability |
| Finance Manager | Financial results, receivables, expenses, and cash flow |
| Accounting Manager | Transaction accuracy and process efficiency |
| Sales Manager | Sales trends and customer performance |
| Marketing Manager | Campaign effectiveness and conversion performance |
| Operations Manager | Workflow performance and bottlenecks |
| Executive Leadership | Strategic KPIs and organizational performance |
| Business Analyst | Requirements, processes, and decision support |

### Stakeholder Questions

- Who requested the analysis?
- What decision are they trying to make?
- What information do they currently use?
- What information is missing?
- Which KPIs matter most?
- How frequently will results be needed?
- What format will be most useful?

For hypothetical case studies, stakeholders may be fictional but should be identified as part of the scenario.

## 8. Defining Analytical Scope

A clear scope prevents a case study from becoming unnecessarily broad.

### Scope Components

**Included:**

- Business function
- Reporting period
- Relevant metrics
- Data categories
- Research questions
- Intended outputs

**Excluded:**

- Unrelated business functions
- Unsupported conclusions
- Unavailable data
- Decisions outside the analytical objective
- Additional questions requiring separate projects

### Example Scope

**Case Study:** Hypothetical Retail Sales Performance

**Included:**

- Monthly revenue
- Product categories
- Units sold
- Gross profit
- Gross profit margin

**Excluded:**

- Customer lifetime value
- Advertising attribution
- Employee performance
- Inventory forecasting

The excluded areas may become separate future studies.

## 9. Preliminary Data Requirements

Business questions determine the information needed for analysis.

| Business Question | Potential Data Requirements |
|---|---|
| Which invoices are overdue? | Invoice date, due date, balance, payment status |
| Which products are most profitable? | Product, revenue, cost, quantity |
| How are expenses changing? | Expense category, amount, date |
| Which marketing channels perform best? | Channel, spend, conversions, attributed revenue |
| Where do processing delays occur? | Process stage, start time, completion time |
| Which customers purchase repeatedly? | Customer identifier, order date, transaction identifier |

### Data Feasibility Questions

- Is the required data available?
- Is the source appropriate?
- Is the information sufficiently complete?
- Are relevant dates included?
- Are definitions consistent?
- Can the data be used legally and ethically?
- Are there important limitations?

At this stage, the goal is to identify data requirements, not necessarily to perform the analysis.

## 10. Business Impact & Prioritization

Not every business problem requires the same level of investigation.

Potential prioritization factors include:

| Factor | Guiding Question |
|---|---|
| Financial Impact | Could the issue affect revenue, cost, or profitability? |
| Operational Impact | Does it affect productivity or workflow performance? |
| Customer Impact | Could it influence satisfaction or retention? |
| Urgency | Does the issue require immediate attention? |
| Data Availability | Is relevant information accessible? |
| Decision Value | Could analysis meaningfully inform a decision? |
| Complexity | Can the problem be investigated within the available time and skills? |
| Learning Value | Does the project support technical and analytical development? |

For beginner portfolio projects, problems with clear metrics, manageable datasets, and practical business relevance are generally preferable to unnecessarily complex scenarios.

## 12. Example Business Problem: Accounts Receivable

### Scenario

Hypothetical service-based company using synthetic invoice data.

### Business Problem

Management is concerned that overdue accounts receivable balances may be increasing, but the company lacks a clear view of aging categories and customer-level exposure.

### Business Objectives

1. Summarize outstanding receivables by aging category.
2. Identify customers with the highest overdue balances.
3. Evaluate changes in overdue balances across reporting periods, if sufficient data is available.

### Research Questions

- What percentage of receivables is overdue?
- Which aging categories contain the largest balances?

- Are overdue balances increasing or decreasing over time?

### Potential KPIs

- Total accounts receivable
- Total overdue balance
- Overdue percentage
- Balances by aging category
- Customer concentration

### Intended Decision

Support management in identifying collection priorities and determining which balances warrant further investigation.

### Potential Tools

Microsoft Excel, SQL, Python, or Power BI.

## 17. Case Study Development Workflow

Business problems form the foundation of a broader analytical process.

**Define the Problem → Establish Objectives → Identify Stakeholders → Develop Research Questions → Assess Data Requirements → Confirm Scope → Prepare Data → Analyze → Interpret → Communicate → Recommend → Document**

### Stage 1: Business Problem Definition

Clarify the issue and intended decision.

### Stage 2: Data Preparation

Identify, obtain, validate, and organize appropriate information.

### Stage 3: Analysis

Apply suitable calculations, comparisons, and analytical methods.

### Stage 4: Interpretation

Explain what the results indicate and what remains uncertain.

### Stage 5: Communication

Present findings through reports, dashboards, or written summaries.

### Stage 6: Recommendations

Develop reasonable actions supported by the available evidence.

### Stage 7: Documentation

Record methods, assumptions, results, limitations, and lessons learned.

Each completed case study should connect these stages into a coherent business narrative.

## 19. Data Security & Ethical Guidelines

Only use information that is appropriate for public portfolio development.

Acceptable sources may include:

- Publicly available datasets with appropriate usage permissions
- Synthetic datasets
- Hypothetical business scenarios
- Properly authorized and anonymized information
- Personal business data that I am permitted to disclose

Do not publish:

- Confidential employer records
- Customer financial information
- Personally identifiable information
- Proprietary company data
- Restricted reports
- Sensitive transaction details

Anonymization alone does not necessarily authorize publication. Data permissions and applicable restrictions must also be considered.
