# 1. Purpose

The purpose of this folder is to develop the ability to analyze financial information and explain what financial statements reveal about business performance.

Financial reporting provides information about:

- Revenue and expenses.
- Profitability.
- Assets and liabilities.
- Equity.
- Cash generation and use.
- Financial obligations.
- Operating performance.
- Financial position.

However, individual financial statements provide only part of the overall picture.

A business may report strong revenue growth while experiencing declining profit margins.

A company may report positive net income while generating negative operating cash flow.

A balance sheet may show substantial assets without demonstrating whether those assets are readily available to meet short-term obligations.

Financial statement analysis connects these different perspectives.

### Central Business Question

**How can financial statements, ratios, and historical comparisons be used together to evaluate profitability, liquidity, financial position, cash flow, and overall business performance?**

# 4. Understanding the Three Financial Statements

Financial statements describe different aspects of an organization's financial condition.

| Statement | Main Question | Reporting Perspective |
|---|---|---|
| Income Statement | Was the business profitable? | Period of time |
| Balance Sheet | What does the business own and owe? | Specific date |
| Cash Flow Statement | How did cash move through the business? | Period of time |

### Important Principle

**Financial statements should be interpreted together because profitability, financial position, and cash flow are related but distinct concepts.**

# 6. Revenue Analysis

## Purpose

Evaluate changes in business revenue across reporting periods.

### Potential Measures

- Total revenue
- Revenue growth
- Revenue by business segment
- Revenue by reporting period

### Revenue Growth Formula

**Revenue Growth % = (Current Revenue − Previous Revenue) ÷ Previous Revenue × 100**

### Illustrative Example

- Previous year revenue: $120,000
- Current year revenue: $150,000

**Revenue Growth = 25%**

### Interpretation

Revenue increased by 25% between the two periods.

Additional analysis is needed to determine whether profitability and cash flow improved.

# 7. Profitability Analysis

## Gross Profit

**Gross Profit = Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold**

## Gross Profit Margin

**Gross Margin % = Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

## Operating Profit

Under a simplified statement:

**Operating Profit = Gross Profit − Operating Expenses**

## Operating Profit Margin

**Operating Margin % = Operating Profit ÷ Revenue × 100**

## Net Profit Margin

**Net Margin % = Net Income ÷ Revenue × 100**

### Potential Business Questions

- How much profit is generated from sales?
- Are direct costs increasing?
- Are operating expenses affecting profitability?
- Are margins improving or declining?

### Important Consideration

Actual financial statements may include additional items and classifications that must be incorporated into the calculations.

# 8. Balance Sheet Analysis

## Purpose

Evaluate financial position at a specific reporting date.

### Primary Components

**Assets**

Resources controlled by the business.

**Liabilities**

Present obligations of the business.

**Equity**

Residual interest in assets after liabilities.

### Accounting Equation

**Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

### Simplified Balance Sheet

| Financial Item | Hypothetical Amount |
|---|---:|
| Cash | $30,000 |
| Accounts Receivable | $20,000 |
| Inventory | $25,000 |
| Other Assets | $75,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **$150,000** |
| Accounts Payable | $15,000 |
| Other Liabilities | $55,000 |
| **Total Liabilities** | **$70,000** |
| **Equity** | **$80,000** |
| **Total Liabilities + Equity** | **$150,000** |

All values are hypothetical.

### Validation

**$150,000 = $70,000 + $80,000**

The simplified balance sheet balances.

# 16. Cash Flow Reconciliation

## Purpose

Confirm that reported cash movements reconcile to changes in cash balances.

### Simplified Formula

**Net Change in Cash = Operating Cash Flow + Investing Cash Flow + Financing Cash Flow**

Additional adjustments, such as exchange-rate effects, may apply.

### Illustrative Example

| Cash Flow Category | Hypothetical Amount |
|---|---:|
| Operating Cash Flow | $35,000 |
| Investing Cash Flow | −$20,000 |
| Financing Cash Flow | −$5,000 |
| **Net Change in Cash** | **$10,000** |

If beginning cash is $20,000:

**Ending Cash = $30,000**

### Important Consideration

The definition of cash and cash equivalents must be consistent with the source financial statements.

# 17. Connecting the Three Statements

Financial statement analysis becomes more useful when relationships between the statements are examined.

### Net Income

Reported on the income statement and incorporated into retained earnings through the equity reconciliation.

### Depreciation

Affects income statement expenses and asset carrying values and is generally added back in operating cash flow under the indirect method.

### Accounts Receivable

Reported on the balance sheet and may affect operating cash flow through changes in working capital.

### Debt

Reported on the balance sheet, while qualifying borrowing and repayment cash movements appear in financing cash flow.

### Capital Expenditures

May increase long-term assets and generally create investing cash outflows when paid in cash.

### Important Principle

The three statements are connected through accounting relationships, but not every change in a balance sheet account corresponds directly to a single cash flow line.

# 18. Financial Ratio Analysis

Financial ratios provide standardized measures for evaluating financial performance.

### Main Ratio Families

| Ratio Family | Analytical Purpose |
|---|---|
| Liquidity | Ability to meet short-term obligations |
| Profitability | Ability to generate earnings |
| Leverage | Financing structure and debt exposure |
| Efficiency | Use of assets and working capital |
| Coverage | Ability to meet selected financial obligations |

Ratios should be interpreted with historical context, industry considerations, and accounting definitions.

---

# 19. Liquidity Ratios

## Current Ratio

**Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities**

### Illustrative Example

- Current assets: $100,000
- Current liabilities: $50,000

**Current Ratio = 2.0**

### Interpretation

The fictional company has $2 in current assets for each $1 of current liabilities.

### Quick Ratio

**Quick Ratio = (Cash + Cash Equivalents + Short-Term Marketable Securities + Net Receivables) ÷ Current Liabilities**

### Important Consideration

A strong liquidity ratio does not automatically guarantee that obligations can be paid when due.

---

# 20. Profitability Ratios

### Gross Margin

Gross Profit ÷ Revenue × 100

### Operating Margin

Operating Income ÷ Revenue × 100

### Net Profit Margin

Net Income ÷ Revenue × 100

### Return on Assets

**ROA = Net Income ÷ Average Total Assets × 100**

### Return on Equity

**ROE = Net Income ÷ Average Shareholders' Equity × 100**

### Important Consideration

Average balance sheet values are commonly used when relating period earnings to assets or equity.

---

# 21. Leverage Ratios

## Debt-to-Equity Ratio

**Debt-to-Equity = Defined Total Debt ÷ Total Equity**

### Potential Business Questions

- How much debt financing is used?
- Is financial leverage increasing?
- How does the capital structure change over time?

### Important Consideration

Debt definitions should be documented and applied consistently.

---

# 22. Efficiency Ratios

## Asset Turnover

**Asset Turnover = Net Revenue ÷ Average Total Assets**

## Receivables Turnover

**Receivables Turnover = Net Credit Sales ÷ Average Net Accounts Receivable**

## Inventory Turnover

**Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory**

### Potential Business Questions

- How efficiently are assets used?
- How quickly are receivables collected?
- How frequently is inventory sold?

### Important Consideration

Efficiency ratios should use compatible periods and clearly defined accounting measures.

---

# 23. Coverage Ratios

## Interest Coverage Ratio

A common definition is:

**Interest Coverage = EBIT ÷ Interest Expense**

### Potential Business Question

How much earnings capacity exists relative to interest expense?

### Important Consideration

Coverage calculations vary by definition and should be reconciled to the source financial statements.

---

# 24. Horizontal Financial Statement Analysis

## Purpose

Compare financial statement amounts across reporting periods.

### Formula

**Percentage Change = (Current Period − Base Period) ÷ Base Period × 100**

### Potential Applications

- Revenue growth
- Expense growth
- Asset changes
- Liability changes
- Net income trends

### Important Consideration

Changes involving zero or negative base values require careful interpretation.

---

# 25. Vertical Financial Statement Analysis

## Purpose

Express financial statement items as percentages of a common base.

### Income Statement

**Line Item Percentage = Line Item Amount ÷ Revenue × 100**

### Balance Sheet

**Line Item Percentage = Line Item Amount ÷ Total Assets × 100**

### Potential Business Questions

- What percentage of revenue is consumed by operating expenses?
- What proportion of assets consists of receivables?
- How does financial structure change over time?

# 26. Financial Performance Interpretation

Financial reporting projects should move beyond presenting calculations.

### Interpretation Questions

- What changed?
- How significant was the change?
- Which financial statements provide relevant context?
- Are results consistent across periods?
- What additional information is needed?
- What limitations affect interpretation?

### Example

A fictional company reports higher net income but lower operating cash flow.

Possible areas for investigation include:

- Changes in receivables
- Inventory movements
- Payables changes
- Noncash income or expenses
- Timing differences

The relationship should be investigated rather than treated as proof of a specific cause.

# 30. Illustrative Excel Formulas

Examples demonstrate syntax and should be adapted to actual workbook structures.

### Gross Profit

```excel
=Revenue-COGS
```

### Gross Margin

```excel
=IFERROR(GrossProfit/Revenue,"N/A")
```

### Current Ratio

```excel
=IFERROR(CurrentAssets/CurrentLiabilities,"N/A")
```

### Net Profit Margin

```excel
=IFERROR(NetIncome/Revenue,"N/A")
```

### Balance Sheet Check

```excel
=TotalAssets-(TotalLiabilities+TotalEquity)
```

### Cash Reconciliation

```excel
=EndingCash-BeginningCash-NetCashChange
```

### Important Consideration

A zero reconciliation difference is one validation check, not proof that every underlying financial statement amount is accurate.
