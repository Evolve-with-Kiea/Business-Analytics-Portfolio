# Marketing ROI

## Shop the Evolution™ | Evolve with Kiea | Brand Evolution Marketing Agency

### Marketing Investment, Customer Acquisition Economics & Financial Performance Analysis

## Project Overview

**Marketing ROI** is a planned Business Analytics portfolio project focused on evaluating the financial effectiveness of marketing investments across product-based and service-based businesses.

The project is designed around realistic startup scenarios involving Shop the Evolution™, Evolution Press®, Little Bosses™, Evolve with Kiea, and Brand Evolution Marketing Agency.

Its purpose is to examine how marketing spending relates to customer acquisition, product sales, qualified leads, contribution margins, and potential financial returns.

Unlike campaign performance reporting, which may focus on impressions, clicks, engagement, and conversions, this project emphasizes **the economics of marketing decisions**.

The central challenge is determining whether marketing generates sufficient incremental financial value to justify the costs incurred.

The project will initially use fictional or synthetic datasets to practice calculations and analytical interpretation. Authorized actual business data may be incorporated into private analysis as it becomes available, with appropriate safeguards before anything is published.

**Project Status:** Planned — Not Yet Completed

**Primary Focus:** Marketing financial analysis, customer acquisition economics, business profitability, and investment evaluation

**Potential Tools:** Microsoft Excel, Power Query, SQL, Python, and Power BI

**Initial Deliverable:** Excel marketing investment and profitability analysis workbook

**Future Deliverable:** Interactive marketing financial performance dashboard

# 2. Business Problem

Startup businesses frequently have limited marketing budgets, incomplete historical information, and uncertain customer acquisition costs.

Marketing activities may generate traffic, inquiries, and sales without producing sufficient financial return.

For example:

- A product campaign may generate sales while leaving little contribution after product and marketing costs.
- A book promotion may produce royalties that are lower than promotional spending.
- A service campaign may generate inexpensive inquiries that rarely become paying clients.
- A newsletter campaign may attract subscribers without immediate purchases.
- An organic content strategy may have little advertising spend but require substantial production time.

Without a consistent analytical framework, a business owner may mistake sales volume, engagement, or platform-reported return on ad spend for profitability.

### Central Business Question

**How can the Evolve with Kiea business ecosystem evaluate marketing investments, customer acquisition costs, conversion value, and payback to support financially responsible startup growth?**

# 4. Financial Measurement Principles

Marketing financial analysis requires careful separation of related but different measures.

| Measure | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Advertising Spend | Amount paid to advertising platforms |
| Total Marketing Investment | Defined marketing-related costs included in the analysis |
| Attributed Revenue | Revenue assigned to marketing activity under a documented attribution method |
| Incremental Revenue | Additional revenue caused by marketing relative to an appropriate counterfactual |
| Gross Profit | Revenue less the applicable cost of goods sold or direct service delivery costs |
| Contribution | Revenue less the defined variable costs |
| Marketing ROI | Net incremental financial benefit relative to defined marketing investment |
| Cash Receipts | Actual payments collected |
| Accounting Profit | Revenue less recognized expenses under the applicable accounting basis |

**Important:** Attributed revenue is not automatically incremental revenue, and revenue is not equivalent to profit or cash received.

# 6. Core Marketing Financial KPIs

| KPI | Formula | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Cost Per Lead | Defined lead acquisition cost ÷ attributed leads | Evaluate lead generation cost |
| Cost Per Qualified Lead | Defined cost ÷ qualified leads | Evaluate cost of relevant prospects |
| Customer Acquisition Cost | Defined acquisition cost ÷ new customers acquired | Evaluate customer acquisition economics |
| Cost Per Purchase | Defined campaign cost ÷ attributed purchases | Evaluate purchase acquisition cost |
| Return on Ad Spend | Attributed revenue ÷ advertising spend | Measure attributed revenue per advertising dollar |
| Contribution Margin % | Contribution ÷ revenue × 100 | Measure contribution after defined variable costs |
| Contribution-Based Marketing ROI | (Incremental contribution before marketing − marketing investment) ÷ marketing investment × 100 | Evaluate return using incremental contribution |
| Acquisition Payback Period | Acquisition cost ÷ periodic contribution from an acquired customer | Estimate recovery time under stated assumptions |

Calculations should be omitted or labeled not applicable when required data is missing or denominators are zero.

# 7. Return on Marketing Investment

## Purpose

Determine whether the incremental economic benefit of a marketing activity exceeds the investment required.

### Preferred Contribution-Based Formula

**Marketing ROI = (Incremental Contribution Before Marketing − Marketing Investment) ÷ Marketing Investment × 100**

This calculation assumes incremental contribution is measured after relevant nonmarketing variable costs but before subtracting the marketing investment being evaluated.

### Illustrative Example

A hypothetical campaign produces:

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Incremental Revenue | $5,000 |
| Incremental Product and Fulfillment Costs | $2,800 |
| Incremental Contribution Before Marketing | $2,200 |
| Marketing Investment | $1,000 |
| Net Incremental Contribution After Marketing | $1,200 |

**Marketing ROI = ($2,200 − $1,000) ÷ $1,000 × 100 = 120%**

This means the estimated net incremental contribution is 120% of the defined marketing investment.

### Important Limitation

The example assumes that the additional revenue was genuinely caused by marketing. In practice, establishing incrementality may require controlled experiments or other defensible methods.

If only platform-attributed revenue is available, the result should be labeled as an attributed-return estimate rather than a proven incremental ROI.

---

# 8. Return on Ad Spend Versus Marketing ROI

ROAS and ROI answer different questions.

### Illustrative Example

A campaign produces $4,000 in attributed revenue from $1,000 in advertising spend.

**ROAS = $4,000 ÷ $1,000 = 4.0**

However, suppose the related product and fulfillment costs are $2,500.

Attributed contribution before advertising is $1,500.

After the $1,000 advertising spend, the remaining attributed contribution is $500.

The illustrative contribution-based return on advertising spending is:

**($1,500 − $1,000) ÷ $1,000 × 100 = 50%**

This is an attributed contribution-based estimate, not proof of incremental marketing ROI.

### Analytical Lesson

A high ROAS does not guarantee a high profit margin or positive incremental return.

# 9. Customer Acquisition Cost

## Purpose

Evaluate the resources required to acquire new paying customers.

### Formula

**CAC = Defined Customer Acquisition Costs ÷ New Customers Acquired**

### Illustrative Example

A startup spends $900 on defined acquisition activities and acquires 15 new customers.

**CAC = $900 ÷ 15 = $60**

### Important Considerations

- Define the acquisition period.
- Identify included costs.
- Count new customers consistently.
- Exclude existing customers when calculating new-customer CAC.
- Avoid combining leads with paying customers.
- Consider delayed conversions.
- Document whether acquisition costs include labor and overhead.

### Business Questions

- Is acquisition cost sustainable relative to customer contribution?
- Which channels acquire customers more efficiently?
- How does CAC vary by product or service?
- Does customer retention improve long-term acquisition economics?

# 10. Cost Per Lead & Lead Quality

A low cost per lead does not necessarily indicate efficient customer acquisition.

### Illustrative Example

| Measure | Campaign A | Campaign B |
|---|---:|---:|
| Marketing Cost | $300 | $400 |
| Leads | 30 | 20 |
| Cost Per Lead | $10 | $20 |
| Customers Acquired | 2 | 5 |
| Cost Per Acquired Customer | $150 | $80 |

Campaign A generates less expensive leads.

Campaign B acquires paying customers at a lower cost.

### Interpretation

Lead quality and downstream conversion should be evaluated alongside cost per lead.

This is particularly relevant to future Brand Evolution Marketing Agency campaigns, where introductory conversations and proposals may be more meaningful than inquiry volume alone.

**Newsletter Signup:** May not have an immediately measurable financial value.

### Expected Lead Value

A simplified planning estimate may be:

**Expected Lead Contribution = Probability of Becoming a Customer × Expected Contribution per Customer**

This is a forecast, not realized revenue.

# 16. Customer Acquisition Payback

## Purpose

Estimate how long it may take to recover customer acquisition costs from customer contribution.

### Simplified Formula

**Payback Period = CAC ÷ Average Monthly Contribution per Acquired Customer**

### Illustrative Example

A hypothetical service business incurs a CAC of $240.

An acquired client generates an average of $80 in monthly contribution after defined service delivery costs.

**Payback Period = $240 ÷ $80 = 3 months**

### Assumptions

This simplified calculation assumes:

- Stable monthly contribution
- Continued customer activity
- Consistent cost definitions
- No additional acquisition costs
- No material timing differences

Where contribution varies by month, cumulative contribution should be used to determine the actual modeled payback period.

### Important Limitation

Contribution-based payback is not automatically the same as cash payback.

Cash payback must account for actual collection and payment timing.

# 17. Marketing Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis can help estimate the minimum incremental sales or contribution required to recover marketing spending.

### Simplified Formula

**Break-Even Incremental Orders = Marketing Investment ÷ Contribution per Incremental Order Before Marketing**

Round up to the next whole order.

### Illustrative Example

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Marketing Investment | $300 |
| Contribution per Incremental Order | $15 |
| Break-Even Incremental Orders | 20 |

The business would need 20 incremental orders at the stated contribution level to cover the defined marketing investment.

### Important Considerations

- Returns
- Discounts
- Shipping
- Product mix
- Repeat purchases
- Attribution uncertainty
- Incrementality

# 19. Marketing Attribution & Incrementality

Attribution is one of the most important limitations in marketing financial analysis.

### Attribution Questions

- Which marketing touchpoint receives credit?
- What attribution window is used?
- Are view-through conversions included?
- Can multiple platforms claim the same purchase?
- Are organic and paid channels being distinguished?
- Are returning customers included?
- Is conversion tracking complete?

### Attribution Versus Incrementality

**Attribution** assigns credit for observed conversions.

**Incrementality** attempts to estimate which conversions occurred because of the marketing activity rather than what would have happened anyway.

Attribution alone does not establish causation.

### Potential Methods for Stronger Evaluation

- Controlled experiments
- Holdout groups
- Geographic testing
- Before-and-after comparisons with appropriate controls
- Sensitivity analysis

These are potential future analytical methods and should not be represented as completed experiments.
